# Fake customer support accounts: how impersonators target your customers

Counterfeits steal sales. Fake support accounts steal from your customers
directly, and the customer usually blames you.

The scam is simple. A customer has a problem with an order and complains about it
in public, often by tagging your brand on social media. Within minutes an account
with your logo and a name like "YourBrand Support" or "YourBrand Help Desk" replies,
says sorry, and asks the customer to continue in a direct message, on a messaging
app or on a "support form". What happens next depends on what the impersonator
wants.

## What the impersonator is after

- **Payment details.** A "refund" or "replacement shipping fee" that needs a card
  number, or a payment link to a page the impersonator controls.
- **Account credentials.** A link to a "verification" page that copies your login
  screen. This overlaps with
  [brand phishing pages](./08-brand-phishing-pages.md); the support account is only
  the delivery route.
- **One-time codes.** The customer is asked to read back a code "to confirm their
  identity". The code is really the second factor for the customer's own account
  somewhere else.
- **Direct payment.** A request to pay for a "priority resolution" with a method
  that can't be reversed.

In each case the impersonator relies on two things: the customer already has a real
problem, and the customer reached out first, so a reply feels expected rather than
suspicious.

## Why brands are slow to notice

Fake support accounts tend to stay invisible to the brand for a while:

- They reply in threads your social team may never open: replies to customers, not
  posts on your own profile.
- Their profile names and handles are built to look right at a glance and to slip
  past a search for your exact handle: an extra word, an underscore, a swapped letter.
- Some are deleted after a few days and replaced, so by the time a customer reports
  one, it may be gone.

Often the first sign is a support ticket from a customer who's angry about a refund
they "already discussed with your team".

## How to find them

You don't need special tools to start. Set aside half an hour and check:

1. **Replies to your mentions.** Go through recent public complaints that tag your
   brand and look at every account that replied. Anything using your name or logo
   that isn't one of your accounts is a finding.
2. **Handle and display-name variants.** Search each platform for your brand name
   combined with *support*, *help*, *care*, *service*, *official*, *team*, and with
   common separators (underscore, dot, no space). Display names on many platforms
   don't have to be unique, so search those as well as handles.
3. **Your logo.** A reverse image search on your logo and your support avatar turns
   up profiles that reuse them.
4. **Linked domains.** Look at the link in each suspect account's bio or messages.
   It often points to a newly registered lookalike domain, which is a separate
   finding with its own fix (see
   [lookalike domains](./03-lookalike-domains-typosquatting.md)).

Write down what you find: profile URL, handle, display name, the date you saw it, and
screenshots of the profile and of any reply to a customer. Accounts disappear and
platforms want specifics. The [evidence file](./09-takedown-evidence-file.md) covers
the details.

## How to get them removed

Every major social platform bans impersonation and has a reporting process for it.
The forms and their names change, so check each platform's current help centre. The
general shape is consistent:

| What to report | Route that usually fits | What you will typically need |
|---|---|---|
| Account pretending to be your company | Platform's impersonation report | Link to the fake account, link to your real account, proof you represent the company |
| Account using your registered logo or name | Platform's trademark report | Trademark registration details and the account link |
| Account posting your copyrighted images | Platform's copyright report | The original images and where they first appeared |
| Link to a phishing or payment page | Platform report *and* the host/registrar of the linked domain | The linked URL and a screenshot of the page |

Some practical points:

- **Report from the brand's own verified account where the platform allows it.**
  Reports that clearly come from the impersonated party tend to be taken more
  seriously than reports from bystanders.
- **Report the destination as well as the account.** Removing a profile while the
  phishing page it linked to stays up only means the next fake account uses the
  same link.
- **Keep the report IDs.** Fake support accounts get recreated, and showing a pattern
  helps the next report.

## Make the real account easy to recognise

You can't stop people creating fake accounts, but you can make your real ones easier
to tell apart.

- **Get verified** wherever the platform offers verification to organisations.
- **Say plainly how you'll contact customers.** One sentence in your bio and on your
  help page: "We will never ask for your password, card number or a one-time code in
  a DM." Then stick to it, including in your own support process.
- **Reply in public first.** If your real team always replies publicly before moving
  to DMs, a fake that goes straight to DMs looks out of place.
- **Use one support handle per platform**, and list them on your website. Customers
  can then check a handle against that list.
- **Tell your support team what to look for.** A customer who mentions a refund that
  nobody on your team arranged is a lead. Ask for the account name and add it to the
  list.

## When a customer has already been hurt

Some customers will lose money before you find the account. You owe them a fast,
plain response:

1. Tell them you didn't contact them and say which account did.
2. Point them to their bank or card issuer to dispute the payment, and to change any
   password they entered.
3. Report the account and the linked page straight away, and keep the evidence.
4. Consider posting a short public note if several customers were targeted. It costs
   little and helps the customers who haven't written in yet.

This is a customer-care issue as much as a brand-protection one. How you handle
these conversations decides whether the customer blames the scammer or you.

## Make it a routine

Impersonation accounts come and go faster than most counterfeit listings, so a single
clean-up pass doesn't last. Fold the checks above into whatever routine you already
have for finding counterfeits and lookalike domains. The same weekly pass can cover
all three, because the three often lead to each other: a fake support account links
to a lookalike domain, and the lookalike domain sells counterfeits.
