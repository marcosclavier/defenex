When a lookalike domain is causing real harm, abuse reports to the host and
registrar can get the content taken down. But the domain stays with its owner,
who can point it at a new host next week. If you want the domain itself, the
standard route is the Uniform Domain-Name Dispute-Resolution Policy — the UDRP.

It's faster and much cheaper than a lawsuit, and it's a paper process: no
hearings, no discovery. It's also narrower than people expect. This article
covers what it can do, what you have to prove, and how to decide whether a given
domain is worth it.

*This is an overview of how the process works, not legal advice. Most
complainants use a trademark lawyer, and for good reason.*

## What the UDRP is

Every registrar accredited by ICANN for generic top-level domains — `.com`,
`.net`, `.org`, and the newer extensions like `.shop` and `.store` — must agree
to the UDRP, and every person registering a domain through them agrees to it as
part of the registration contract. That's what gives it teeth: the registrar is
bound to carry out the decision.

Many country-code domains have adopted the UDRP too, but some run their own
policies — `.uk`, for example, has Nominet's own dispute resolution service.
Check which policy applies to the extension before you start.

Complaints are filed with an approved dispute-resolution provider. WIPO's
Arbitration and Mediation Center handles the most cases; the Forum is the other
large one, and there are a few regional providers.

## What it can and can't do

The only remedies are **transfer** of the domain to you, or **cancellation**. No
damages, no costs awarded against the other side, no order covering future
registrations. If the same person registers a different lookalike tomorrow,
that's a new complaint.

## The three things you have to prove

The complainant must establish all three. Missing any one loses the case.

1. **The domain is identical or confusingly similar to a trademark in which you
   have rights.** A registered mark is the cleanest basis. Unregistered
   (common-law) rights can qualify, but you'll need evidence the name has acquired
   distinctiveness, which is more work. The comparison is usually just the
   domain's text against the mark; adding a generic word such as "outlet" or
   "shop" generally doesn't avoid confusing similarity.
2. **The registrant has no rights or legitimate interests in the domain.** You
   make a prima facie case — they're not known by that name, you never licensed
   them, they're not making a legitimate non-commercial or fair use — and the
   burden shifts to them to show otherwise.
3. **The domain was registered and is being used in bad faith.** The policy gives
   examples: registering mainly to sell the domain to you or a competitor,
   registering to block you from using it, registering to disrupt a competitor's
   business, or using it to attract users for commercial gain by creating
   confusion with your mark.

The third element is where most complaints are won or lost, and where evidence
matters most. A domain running a counterfeit store with your product photos is a
strong case. A domain that's been parked for years without content is harder,
though not impossible: panels have found passive holding to be in bad faith in
some circumstances, such as when the mark is well known and there's no plausible
good-faith use.

Timing matters too. If the domain was registered *before* you had any trademark
rights, it's hard to argue it was registered in bad faith against you.

## What it costs

At WIPO, the provider fee for a complaint covering one to five domains decided
by a single panelist is **$1,500**; a three-member panel costs more. Other
providers' fees are in a similar range. Check the provider's current fee schedule
before filing.

Legal fees come on top. A clear-cut case with good evidence is not much drafting
work for a specialist; a contested one with a responding party and counsel is
more.

## How long it takes

Roughly two months from filing to decision is typical when the respondent doesn't
engage, which is common. The broad sequence:

1. You file the complaint; the provider checks it for compliance and the
   registrar locks the domain.
2. The respondent is notified and has 20 days to respond.
3. A panel is appointed and issues a decision.
4. If you win, the registrar implements the transfer after a short waiting period
   (10 business days), unless the respondent shows they've started court
   proceedings.

## Is this domain worth it? A practical test

Before spending $1,500 plus fees, ask:

- **Is it doing harm now?** A live counterfeit store, a phishing page or a fake
  support site is worth acting on. A parked typo with no traffic usually isn't —
  monitor it instead. See [Lookalike domains](./03-lookalike-domains-typosquatting.md)
  for how to tell them apart.
- **Would removing the content be enough?** If the operator is likely to move on
  once the site is down, a registrar or host abuse report may solve the problem
  for free. UDRP makes sense when the *domain* is the asset — it's highly
  confusing, it keeps getting reused, or customers have already learned it.
- **Can you prove all three elements?** Particularly bad faith. If your only
  evidence is that the domain exists, wait until it's used, and capture that
  use when it happens. [The evidence file](./09-takedown-evidence-file.md)
  explains what to keep.
- **Is URS an option?** For many newer extensions, the Uniform Rapid Suspension
  System is quicker and cheaper. It only suspends the domain for the rest of its
  registration rather than transferring it, and it needs a clearer case, but
  for an obvious abuse site that may be all you need.
- **Is the registration older than your mark?** If so, get advice before filing.

## What to have ready

If you do decide to file, your lawyer — or you — will need:

- Trademark registration certificates, with dates, for every jurisdiction you rely on.
- The domain's registration date and registrar, from WHOIS or RDAP.
- Dated captures of what the domain has shown: full-page screenshots, saved
  copies, archive links.
- Any evidence of confusion: customer emails, support tickets, orders placed on
  the wrong site.
- Any contact you've had with the registrant, including offers to sell.

Collecting this as you go is much easier than reconstructing it later.

## Don't overreach

Panels can find that a complaint was brought in bad faith — "reverse domain name
hijacking" — when a complainant tries to use the UDRP to take a domain it has no
real claim to, for example from someone using a common word legitimately. The
finding has no financial penalty, but it's published with your name on it. File
when the three elements are clearly there, not to pressure someone into a sale.
