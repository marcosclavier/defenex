Most brands find out about counterfeits from a customer. Someone emails support
about a product that broke in a week, or arrived without the usual packaging, and
when you ask for the order number it isn't one of yours.

By then the listing has been live for a while. This article is about finding it
first. Everything below can be done by one person with a browser and an
afternoon. It won't be exhaustive, but it will tell you whether you have a
problem, and roughly how big.

## Before you search: write down what "yours" looks like

The hardest part of a counterfeit search isn't finding listings. It's deciding
which ones aren't yours. Before you start, make a short list:

- **Your official domains**, including regional ones and any old campaign domains.
- **Your official marketplace storefronts** — the exact seller names on Amazon,
  eBay, Etsy or wherever you sell.
- **Authorized resellers and distributors**, with their domains and seller names.
- **Your official social accounts**, including support handles.
- **Your retail price floor.** If the cheapest legitimate price for your product is
  $80, a new-in-box listing at $19 is worth a closer look.

Without this list you'll spend most of your time re-discovering your own
distributors. That's the most common false positive in brand protection, and the
most embarrassing one to send to a lawyer.

## Step 1: search engines, with operators

Start with Google, because that's where your customers start. Plain searches
for your brand name mostly return your own pages, so use operators to strip
them out:

```
"yourbrand" -site:yourbrand.com
"yourbrand" replica
"yourbrand" wholesale
"yourbrand" cheap outlet
"yourbrand" 1:1
```

The words that matter are the ones counterfeit sellers use to describe their own
stock: *replica*, *AAA*, *1:1*, *mirror quality*, *factory direct*, *wholesale*,
*OEM*. Try each with your brand and your best-selling product names.

Then look at the shopping results and the ads, not just the organic links. A
shopping carousel showing your product from a store you've never heard of, at
a third of your price, is a live commercial offer — more urgent than a blog post
that mentions you. Ads bidding on your brand name are a separate signal worth
noting.

Repeat a few of the searches with a different country set in Google's settings
or a different regional Google domain. Counterfeit storefronts often target one
market and are invisible from another.

## Step 2: the marketplaces directly

Search engines index marketplaces unevenly. Go to each one and search inside it.

| Where | What to search | What to look for |
|---|---|---|
| Amazon | Brand name, product names, model numbers | Sellers not on your list; "new" condition far below retail |
| eBay | Same, sorted by lowest price | Bulk lots; sellers shipping many units from one location |
| Etsy | Brand name + "inspired", product names | Your logo on handmade items; listings using your photos |
| AliExpress, DHgate, Alibaba | Brand name, "replica" + category | Wholesale quantities; your product photos with a different seller |
| Walmart Marketplace, Temu, Shein | Brand and product names | Same signals as Amazon |

Two shortcuts help:

- **Reverse image search your product photos.** Counterfeit sellers rarely shoot
  their own. Google Lens or Bing Visual Search on your main product image will
  often turn up the listing faster than a text search does.
- **Search your model numbers and SKUs.** They're specific enough that almost
  every result is relevant.

## Step 3: domains

Counterfeit stores frequently run on their own domains, and the names tend to
follow patterns: your brand plus *outlet*, *sale*, *store*, *official*, or a
country code. Search for those combinations directly, and check whether
`yourbrand-outlet.com` and similar names actually resolve.

Lookalike domains deserve their own process — misspellings, swapped characters,
other top-level domains. We cover that in
[Lookalike domains: how typosquatting works](./03-lookalike-domains-typosquatting.md).

## Step 4: social

Search Instagram, Facebook, TikTok and X for your brand name and obvious
variants (`yourbrand_official`, `yourbrand.support`, `yourbrandoutlet`). You're
looking for two different things:

- **Shops**: accounts selling your product, often with a link out to a store or
  a "DM to order" line.
- **Impersonators**: accounts posing as you, particularly as customer support.
  These target people who complained about you publicly. See
  [Fake customer support accounts](./07-fake-customer-support-accounts.md).

Also check Facebook's Ad Library for your brand name. Ads run by pages that aren't
yours, using your product images, are a common way counterfeit stores get traffic.

## Step 5: decide what each result actually is

For every result you keep, open the page and ask three questions:

1. **Is something being sold, or just mentioned?** Reviews, news, comparisons and
   forum posts are not infringement. Leave them alone.
2. **Is the seller on your authorized list?** If yes, it's not a finding — even if
   the price is odd.
3. **What specifically on the page makes you think it's a fake?** Write that down
   as a direct quote: "Replica — same as original, 1:1 quality", or a price, or a
   seller location.

The third question is the one that matters. If you can't point to specific text
or images on the page, you have a suspicion, not a finding, and no platform will
act on a suspicion. It's also the habit that keeps you from accusing a legitimate
reseller.

Sort what's left into rough buckets: counterfeits, lookalike domains, phishing,
impersonation, and genuine goods sold through channels you didn't approve. They
go through different enforcement routes, and the last one often isn't an
infringement at all.

## Step 6: capture it before you act

Listings disappear — sometimes because the seller rotates them, sometimes
because they notice you looking. Before you report anything, take a full-page
screenshot with the URL and date visible, save the page, and note the seller
name. If you send a report and the listing comes back under a new name, this is
what lets you show it's the same operation.

## The limits of doing this by hand

A manual search is a snapshot. It tells you what's live this afternoon, from the
searches you thought of, in the markets you checked. Three things it does badly:

- **Coverage.** Search engines return a limited number of results per query, and
  you'll run a few dozen queries at most. Infringement in long-tail results or
  other markets stays invisible.
- **Repetition.** Counterfeit sellers relist. The search you did in March tells
  you nothing about April — more on that in
  [Why counterfeiters relist](./06-why-counterfeiters-relist.md).
- **Triage time.** Most of the afternoon goes on opening pages and deciding
  they're fine.

None of that means you shouldn't do it. Doing it once by hand is the best way
to understand what you're dealing with, and whether it's worth paying for help.
